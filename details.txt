A building's HVAC system is just a machine that spends energy to move a room's temperature toward some target. Traditional building management systems (BMS) pick that target using a fixed rule: "21°C from 8am–6pm, 18°C otherwise." That rule is blind — it doesn't know today is unusually warm, that the room will be empty for the next hour, or that electricity is more carbon-intensive right now. It just executes the schedule regardless of what's actually happening. That's the entire problem statement: replace a blind fixed rule with something that senses, reasons, and adapts — while never risking human comfort.

What a "closed loop" fundamentally is
Any control system, however fancy, reduces to three repeating steps:

Sense — read the current state of the world (temperature, occupancy, comfort index)
Decide — figure out what to do about it, given some goal
Act — change something that feeds back into step 1
The word "closed loop" just means step 3 changes the very thing step 1 measures next time — the system watches the consequences of its own actions and corrects course, rather than acting once and hoping.

Why simulate instead of using a real building
You cannot experiment on a real occupied office — a bad decision means someone is actually too cold. You also can't iterate fast on a real building — a day of real time is a day of real time. So you need a physics-based stand-in: EnergyPlus. It's software that models heat transfer, HVAC energy draw, and occupant comfort mathematically, and can run a simulated day in seconds. It gives you a safe, fast, repeatable "world" to sense and act on before (or instead of) touching a real building.

Why an LLM as the "decide" step
The old approach hard-codes the decision logic ("if occupied, 21°C"). The bet here is: instead of a programmer pre-writing every rule, give a language model the current state and a goal in plain English ("balance comfort and energy, prioritizing comfort"), and let it reason out the setpoint each cycle. This is valuable specifically because real goals are multi-objective and context-dependent ("it's about to be occupied, so start warming now" is a judgment call, not a fixed rule) — that's the kind of reasoning LLMs are comparatively good at versus a static if/else tree.

What "tool calling" actually is, mechanically
An LLM by default just emits text. Text alone can't safely control a simulation — you can't eval() free-form model output and trust it. Tool calling is the mechanism that constrains the model's output into a fixed, parseable shape: instead of "let me lower the temperature," it must emit something structurally like set_zone_setpoint(setpoint_c=20.5). Your code is the only thing that actually executes anything — the model can only ever choose which pre-defined, pre-vetted action to take and with what argument. That's the entire safety boundary: the model proposes, your code (not the model) disposes.

This is why tools.py hard-clamps every setpoint to 18–26°C regardless of what's requested — the model's judgment is trusted for what to try, never for the safety limit. That distinction (model chooses, code enforces limits) is the load-bearing safety idea of the whole project.

Why we built a fake building before the real one
Two unknowns at once are hard to debug: "is my LLM agent logic broken?" and "is my EnergyPlus wiring broken?" tangled together waste enormous time — a bug could be either. So tools.py's MockBuilding is a deliberately crude stand-in: a few lines of math (temperature drifts toward a blend of outdoor temp and setpoint; energy is proportional to how hard the HVAC works). It exposes the exact same interface (get_zone_state, set_zone_setpoint) that real EnergyPlus will. This lets you validate "does the agent reason sensibly and call tools correctly?" completely independent of "does EnergyPlus's API actually respond the way I expect?" Once the first is proven, swapping the mock for the real simulator is a wiring change, not a redesign.

How the pieces you now have fit together
run_loop.py           orchestrates: pick a mode, run N timesteps, log every step to CSV
   |
   +-- mock/baseline mode: fixed rule (21°C occupied / 18°C setback) -> MockBuilding
   +-- mock-ai mode:       llm_agent.py's HVACAgent -> MockBuilding
                              |
                              +-- get_zone_state()  (sense)
                              +-- LLM reasons        (decide)
                              +-- set_zone_setpoint() (act, clamped)
dashboard/generate_report.py   compares two logs -> % kWh saved, comfort violations
The baseline run and the AI run use the identical physics model, differing only in who's picking the setpoint. That's what makes the comparison meaningful — any kWh difference is attributable to the decision logic, not to a different simulated building.
