The problem: buildings are climate-controlled by guesswork frozen in time

Start here with zero assumptions. By the end you'll know what a "setpoint" and a "deadband" actually are, and why that 1.7 °C gap in this project's baseline building is the whole reason there's anything to save.

What a thermostat actually does

A room has two numbers controlling it, not one:

heating_cbelow this, the heater switches ON
cooling_cabove this, the AC switches ON

Between those two numbers is the deadband. Inside it, nothing runs — the room just floats wherever the weather and the people in it put it, and that costs zero electricity. Equipment only turns on when the room crosses one of the two thresholds.

First-principles takeaway A setpoint is a threshold, not a "target temperature you're trying to hit." Confusing the two is the single biggest misconception this whole project has to correct — you'll see it again in Lesson 5, because it's exactly the mistake the AI model made in an early version.

Why the deadband width is the whole game

A narrow deadband forces the heater and AC to fight to hold a tight band — expensive, and in bad cases they can work against each other. A wide deadband means the room floats free for longer before anything switches on — cheap. So: the wider you can make the deadband without making anyone uncomfortable, the less electricity you use. That's the entire optimization problem, stated in one sentence.

The actual building in this project

This isn't a toy model. It's 5ZoneAirCooled, a standard example building shipped with EnergyPlus (the industry-grade building physics simulator this project runs on) — five conditioned office zones plus one unconditioned return plenum, VAV (variable-air-volume) system with reheat, simulated against real Chicago summer weather (TMY3 — a "typical meteorological year" weather file), 1–21 July.

The building's factory-default schedule holds an occupied deadband of just 22.2–23.9 °C — only 1.7 °C wide. That's the "guesswork frozen in time" in this lesson's title: someone set that schedule once, and it now runs forever, blind to today's weather and blind to whether anyone is actually in the room right now. That narrowness is deliberate on the project's part, too — it's what leaves room to demonstrate savings.

The actual objective

↓ kWhuse less electricity
= comfortwithout pushing occupied zones outside 20–25 °C

Comfort is measured as the share of occupied zone-readings that fall outside 20–25 °C. Two things worth noticing already: comfort only matters when someone is present (the empty-building hours are free real estate for savings), and this is a hold-both-at-once problem — you're not allowed to trade comfort for savings, you have to find savings that don't cost any comfort at all.

Why this needs to reason, not just execute a schedule

A fixed schedule can't answer any of these questions, every single day:

  • Is it a mild day or a scorcher? A wide deadband is free on a mild day and risky on a hot one.
  • Is the building actually occupied right now, or did everyone leave early?
  • What's the cheapest way to prepare for the next few hours given what just happened?

Answering those well, continuously, is a control problem — and this project's core idea (Lesson 2) is to put a small AI model in charge of answering it, instead of a fixed schedule or a human revisiting it occasionally.

Quick check: why does comfort only get measured during occupied hours?
Because nobody can be made uncomfortable in an empty room — so restricting the equipment there would only waste electricity for no benefit. Unoccupied hours are exactly where the biggest, safest savings live, because the comfort constraint doesn't apply.
