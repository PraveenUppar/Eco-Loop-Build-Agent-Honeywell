Drawbacks and improvements

Every limitation here is one the project itself states openly — that's a deliberate stance worth naming in an interview: an honest limitations section is evidence of rigor, not weakness. Know these cold; they're the questions most likely to come as "but what about—".

One building, one climate, three summer weeks

Nothing in the reported results shows the policy generalizes to another building, another climate, or another season. This is the broadest, most important caveat — everything else below is a special case of it.

The savings ceiling is low by construction

With comfort held at 20–25 °C, roughly 2.5–3.5% of facility electricity is available to save, full stop — that's a property of the comfort constraint, not of how good the controller is. Letting zones drift to 27 °C would reach ~7–7.5%, but breaks comfort, so it's correctly not claimed. Interview framing: if pushed on "why aren't the savings bigger," the honest answer is the ceiling, not the controller.

A 3B model is inconsistent run-to-run

Already the subject of Lessons 5 and 7 — the live agent (3.32%) trails the median-filtered exported IDF (3.79%) precisely because of this. Run-to-run variance is comparable in size to the effect of prompt changes, which is itself informative: it means a lot of apparent "improvement" from prompt tweaking could just be noise unless measured carefully across multiple runs. src/compare_models.py exists specifically to isolate this by running the identical loop against a different local model — though the larger Ollama cloud models need a paid subscription, so that comparison isn't included in the reported results.

Facility percentages are diluted by lights and plug loads

Already covered in Lesson 7 — restated here because it's a drawback of the measurement, not the controller: ~70% of facility electricity is simply outside what a thermostat can ever touch, so the facility-wide number will always look smaller than the HVAC-only number no matter how good the policy gets.

No weather forecast — the clearest next improvement

The agent reacts to current conditions only. It cannot pre-cool a building ahead of a forecast hot afternoon, which a real BMS with forecast data could do to shift load to cheaper/cooler hours. This is explicitly flagged as the most promising next step, not a hidden gap.

Comfort is a temperature band, not PMV

The reported comfort metric is simply "is the zone temperature between 20 and 25 °C" — not a Predicted Mean Vote (PMV) model, which is the standard way thermal comfort is actually modeled in the field (ASHRAE 55). PMV accounts for humidity, air speed, clothing, and metabolic rate, not just air temperature. The model's People objects in the IDF don't specify a Fanger comfort model, and adding one was explicitly out of scope for this project.

A risk not fully tested: winter behavior

Flagged, not claimed The operating envelope is tuned for summer. The unoccupied setback cap (heating ≤ 19 °C) is actually tighter than the baseline's own 16.7 °C night setback — meaning in a winter run, the agent would likely lose to baseline on heating savings. This is stated directly in SUBMISSION.md's known limitations as "the first thing to fix," not discovered by testing it and hiding a bad result.

Roadmap, if this were to continue

ImprovementWhat it would take
Weather-forecast-aware pre-coolingFeed a forecast into the prompt or a dedicated pre-cool decision rule; biggest expected upside.
Multi-building / multi-climate validationRepeat the same 3-mode experiment on other EnergyPlus example buildings and other TMY3 climate files.
Winter validation and re-tuned setback limitsRun the identical pipeline over a heating-dominated period; likely needs the setback cap loosened.
PMV-based comfortAdd a Fanger comfort model to the IDF's People objects and switch the metric from a temperature band to PMV.
Larger-model comparisonRun compare_models.py against a paid cloud model to quantify exactly how much of the savings gap to the exported IDF is model-size-limited.
Quick check: an interviewer asks "why is the savings number so small — only 3%?" What's the strongest honest answer?
Because it's not actually small relative to what's achievable under the stated constraint: with comfort held to 20–25 °C, the entire available ceiling is ~2.5–3.5%, and the agent captures essentially all of it (3.32%, vs. 3.79% for the noise-filtered export). Bigger numbers exist (~7%) only by loosening comfort, which the project explicitly refuses to claim. The right response reframes "small number" as "near the ceiling of an honestly-scoped constraint," backed by the actual ceiling figure.
